CAGR is a straight line between two dots

CAGR = (End / Start)1/years − 1. It answers only: “If this lumpsum had grown at a constant rate, what rate?” It does not see the crash in year two or the bounce in year five. A fund that went ₹1 lakh → ₹2 lakh in twelve months and then sat still for four years can show the same five-year CAGR as a fund that grinded up smoothly.

Period shopping is the usual abuse. Nifty from a market low to a high looks heroic; the same index from a previous high looks dull. Always write the start date and end date next to the number.

Worked numbers, and the SIP trap

₹1,00,000 → ₹1,61,051 in 5 years is 10.00% CAGR. ₹1,00,000 → ₹1,76,234 in 5 years is about 12.00%. Those two are lumpsum paths. If you actually sent ₹5,000 a month, the “start” is not ₹3,00,000 of contributions and the “end” is not a CAGR problem — cash went in on 60 different dates. Pasting total invested as Start on this page will usually overstate the rate because later instalments had less time in the market.

Another abuse: measuring a fund from 23 March 2020 (COVID low) to a later high, then shopping that CAGR against a category average that used calendar years. Write both dates. If you cannot name them, do not quote the percentage in an office chat.

What this calculator will not do

It will not annualise a SIP, an STP, or a folio with withdrawals. It will not add dividends unless they are already inside End. It will not tell you whether 12% is “good” versus Nifty — that comparison needs the same window on the index. If a YouTube thumbnail says “this fund gave 42% CAGR”, ask: start date, end date, lumpsum or SIP, and whether dividends were reinvested.
