How to Save Maximum Tax as a Salaried Employee India 2026

FY 2026-27: Section 87A, 80C, HRA (four metros only), NPS, and when the old regime still wins

Written by Rizwan Ali · Last updated 21 August 2026 · Numbers use the same FY 2026-27 helpers as our income tax calculator

Tax-saving in FY 2026-27 is not the same playbook as FY 2023-24. The new regime’s Section 87A rebate (with marginal relief) makes income tax nil on taxable income up to ₹12 lakh. For a salaried person that is often about ₹12.75 lakh gross after the ₹75,000 standard deduction. Below that band, stuffing 80C into the old regime can increase tax, because old-regime 87A only zeros tax up to ₹5 lakh taxable.

This guide covers the deductions that still matter on the old regime — 80C, 80D, 80CCD(1B), HRA under Section 10(13A) — with Rule 2A metros named correctly (Delhi, Mumbai, Kolkata, Chennai only). Then it runs three worked comparisons from the same engine as the calculator: ₹8 lakh, ₹12 lakh, and ₹18 lakh. Totals include 4% health and education cess. No surcharge applies at these incomes. These are estimates, not an assessment order. Confirm with a Chartered Accountant before you file.

Section 80C — Save Up to ₹1.5 Lakh (Old Regime Only)

Section 80C allows a deduction of up to ₹1,50,000 from taxable income under the old tax regime. It does not apply on the new regime. For someone who is actually in the 20% old-regime slab, fully using 80C saves ₹31,200 including 4% cess. In the 30% slab the saving is ₹46,800. If new-regime 87A already makes your tax zero, that 80C rupee does not cut tax — it only locks money in a product.

Employee Provident Fund (EPF): Statutory employee PF is often ₹1,800/month (₹21,600/year) when contributions are capped at the ₹15,000 wage ceiling. Some employers deduct 12% on full basic — check your payslip before you “fill” the rest of 80C. Public Provident Fund (PPF): 15-year lock-in; annual contribution ₹500 to ₹1,50,000. ELSS: three-year lock-in with equity market risk. Life insurance premiums, NSC, tax-saving FDs, Sukanya Samriddhi, home-loan principal, and tuition fees for up to two children also qualify, subject to the overall cap.

Plan 80C early in the year only after you have compared both regimes. Buying a low-return endowment in March to “save tax” is a common way to overpay twice: once in product returns, and once if the new regime would have been cheaper.

Section 80D — Health Insurance Deduction

Under the old regime you can claim up to ₹25,000 for premiums for yourself, spouse, and dependent children. An additional ₹25,000 is available for parents under 60, or ₹50,000 if parents are 60 or older. If both you and your parents are senior citizens, the combined ceiling is ₹1,00,000. Preventive health check-ups up to ₹5,000 sit inside these limits, not on top.

80D is not available under the new regime. At incomes where new-regime tax is already nil, the insurance still matters for cover — not for TDS. At higher incomes on the old regime, a ₹15,000 premium in the 30% slab saves ₹4,680 including cess.

Section 80CCD(1B) — Additional NPS Deduction of ₹50,000

NPS offers an extra deduction of up to ₹50,000 under 80CCD(1B), independent of the ₹1.5 lakh 80C cap, old regime only. Combined 80C + 80CCD(1B) can reach ₹2,00,000. At the 30% old-regime slab, ₹50,000 NPS saves about ₹15,600 including cess. Employer NPS under 80CCD(2) is a separate line and can still help on the new regime within notified limits — that is not the same as 80CCD(1B).

NPS is a retirement product: partial equity, lock-in until 60 for most subscribers, 60% lump-sum and 40% annuity at exit under current rules. Use it when the old regime already wins on paper, not as a reflex at ₹10 lakh CTC.

HRA Exemption — Four Metros, Not Six

House Rent Allowance exemption under Section 10(13A) is available only on the old tax regime. The exempt amount is the minimum of three figures: (1) actual HRA received, (2) 50% of basic if you live in a Rule 2A metro — Delhi, Mumbai, Kolkata or Chennai — or 40% of basic in every other city, including Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Pune, Gurugram and Ahmedabad, and (3) rent paid minus 10% of basic (basic + DA if DA is part of salary). Employers in Bengaluru often still pay HRA at 50% of basic; that does not make Bengaluru a tax metro. Full walkthrough: HRA exemption guide.

Worked Bengaluru example (non-metro for HRA): basic ₹30,000/month (₹3,60,000/year), HRA received ₹18,000/month (₹2,16,000), rent ₹20,000/month (₹2,40,000).

  • Actual HRA received = ₹2,16,000
  • 40% of basic = ₹1,44,000
  • Rent − 10% of basic = ₹2,40,000 − ₹36,000 = ₹2,04,000

Exempt HRA = ₹1,44,000 (the minimum). If this employee wrongly used the 50% metro cap, the third-lowest figure would look like ₹1,80,000 — ₹36,000 extra exemption that Rule 2A does not allow. If rent is low, the rent−10% limb can bind first and 40% vs 50% may not change the result; always take the minimum of three.

Submit rent receipts and the landlord’s PAN when annual rent exceeds ₹1 lakh. Living in your own house, or with parents without paying rent, means HRA is fully taxable. Paying rent to parents is only as good as the documentation and their rental-income declaration.

Rahul at ₹8 lakh — new is zero; old is not

Gross salary ₹8,00,000. Basic ₹3,20,000 (40%). HRA received ₹1,28,000. Rent ₹1,20,000 in Pune (40% city). Employee EPF ₹21,600, ELSS ₹50,000, health insurance ₹18,000, NPS 80CCD(1B) ₹50,000.

New: ₹8,00,000 − standard deduction ₹75,000 = ₹7,25,000 taxable. 87A zeros tax up to ₹12 lakh taxable. Total tax ₹0.

Old: HRA exemption = min(₹1,28,000, 40% × ₹3,20,000 = ₹1,28,000, ₹1,20,000 − ₹32,000 = ₹88,000) = ₹88,000. Taxable = ₹8,00,000 − ₹50,000 − ₹71,600 (80C) − ₹18,000 − ₹50,000 − ₹88,000 = ₹5,22,400. Old 87A stops at ₹5 lakh taxable, so slab tax remains. Total tax ₹17,659 (tax ₹16,980 + cess ₹679).

Active tax-saving on the old side made Rahul worse off than doing nothing on the new side. That inversion is the FY 2026-27 trap.

Ankit at ₹12 lakh — staying on old because “that is how tax works”

Ankit, 26, software developer in Hyderabad, ₹12,00,000 gross salary. He told payroll he wanted the old regime because his parents always used 80C. He has statutory EPF of ₹21,600 and no other declared deductions.

New (what a colleague on default new would pay): taxable ₹12,00,000 − ₹75,000 = ₹11,25,000. Below the ₹12 lakh 87A ceiling. Total tax ₹0.

Ankit, old: taxable ₹12,00,000 − ₹50,000 − ₹21,600 = ₹11,28,400. Total tax ₹1,57,061 (tax ₹1,51,020 + cess ₹6,041).

Even if he later stuffed 80C to ₹1.5 lakh, 80D ₹25,000 and Bengaluru-style HRA of ₹1.32 lakh, old taxable would be about ₹8.43 lakh and old tax about ₹84,344 — still far above new’s ₹0. At this income, old wins only if deductions crush taxable to around ₹5 lakh (old 87A). That usually needs a large 24(b) claim plus high documented rent, not ELSS bought in March.

When old still wins: ₹18 lakh, Mumbai rent, home-loan interest

Gross salary ₹18,00,000. Basic ₹7,20,000. HRA received ₹3,60,000 (50% of basic). Rent ₹40,000/month in Mumbai (a true metro). 80C ₹1,50,000, 80D ₹25,000, NPS 80CCD(1B) ₹50,000, Section 24(b) interest ₹2,00,000.

New: taxable ₹17,25,000. Slab tax ₹1,45,000 + cess ₹5,800. Total ₹1,50,800. (Matches the worked ₹18 lakh note on the income tax calculator.)

Old: HRA exemption = min(₹3,60,000, 50% × ₹7,20,000 = ₹3,60,000, ₹4,80,000 − ₹72,000 = ₹4,08,000) = ₹3,60,000. Taxable = ₹18,00,000 − ₹50,000 − ₹1,50,000 − ₹25,000 − ₹50,000 − ₹2,00,000 − ₹3,60,000 = ₹9,65,000. Total tax ₹1,09,720 (tax ₹1,05,500 + cess ₹4,220). Old saves about ₹41,000 in this specific stack.

Same ₹18 lakh with Bengaluru rent (40% cap) of ₹25,000/month, HRA ₹2,88,000, and the same 80C / 80D / NPS / 24(b) is almost a tie: old about ₹1,47,264 vs new ₹1,50,800. City and 24(b) change the winner. Do not copy a blog “₹18 lakh always old.”

Use the new regime when

  • Gross salary is around ₹12.75 lakh or below and you have salary income only
  • You do not have a large 24(b) claim or documented high rent
  • You prefer not to lock money in 80C products just to chase a deduction

Use the old regime when

  • A side-by-side calc shows lower tax after HRA + 80C + 80D + NPS + 24(b)
  • You live in a true HRA metro or pay rent high enough that exemption is large
  • You will actually submit Form 12BB proofs on time

Four Common Tax-Saving Mistakes

Mistake 1: Buying low-return insurance for 80C. Traditional life policies often return 5–6% with long lock-ins. If you are on the old regime and need 80C, term cover plus ELSS or PPF is the usual split. If you are on the new regime, 80C does not reduce tax at all.

Mistake 2: Missing employer NPS (80CCD(2)). Payroll NPS with an employer contribution is a different section from 80CCD(1B). Skipping the window can mean leaving matching contributions on the table even when you correctly choose the new regime.

Mistake 3: Not submitting proofs on time. Declaring HRA and 80C in April but missing January proof deadlines makes the employer tax full HRA and skip deductions. You can often reclaim at ITR, but monthly cash flow still takes the TDS hit.

Mistake 4: Ignoring 87A at ₹12 lakh taxable. If new-regime taxable income is ₹12 lakh or less, tax is nil after rebate. Choosing old “to save tax” at ₹8–12 lakh gross with ordinary EPF and a bit of ELSS is how Ankit’s ₹1.57 lakh bill happens. Always run both columns after every Budget.

Additional Deductions Worth Knowing

On the old regime, also check: Section 24(b) — home-loan interest up to ₹2,00,000 on self-occupied property. Section 80E — education-loan interest, no upper rupee cap. Section 80G — specified donations. Section 80TTA / 80TTB — savings interest up to ₹10,000 (₹50,000 for specified senior citizens). Standard deduction — ₹50,000 old / ₹75,000 new on salary or pension, no extra paperwork. Rules: Income Tax Department; Income-tax Act, 2025 as in force from 1 April 2026. Frequently Asked Questions

Should I choose the old or new tax regime in 2026?

Under FY 2026-27 rules, the new regime plus Section 87A makes tax nil on taxable income up to ₹12 lakh (about ₹12.75 lakh gross salary after the ₹75,000 standard deduction). Choose the new regime if your gross salary is in that band or you have few old-regime deductions. Choose the old regime only if you can document large deductions — typically home-loan interest under Section 24(b), high HRA exemption, full 80C and 80D, and NPS — and a side-by-side calculation still shows a lower tax. Do not assume old wins from ₹8 lakh upward; at ₹8–12 lakh gross the new regime is often zero.

What investments qualify under Section 80C?

Section 80C covers up to ₹1.5 lakh in specified investments and expenses: Employee Provident Fund (employee contribution), Public Provident Fund (PPF), ELSS mutual funds, National Savings Certificate (NSC), tax-saving fixed deposits (5-year lock-in), life insurance premiums, Sukanya Samriddhi Yojana, home loan principal repayment, and tuition fees for up to two children. EPF contributions are automatic for most salaried employees, so factor that in before planning additional investments to reach the ₹1.5 lakh cap. 80C is available only under the old tax regime.

How does HRA exemption reduce my taxable income?

HRA exemption under the old regime is the minimum of three amounts: actual HRA received; 50% of basic for Delhi, Mumbai, Kolkata or Chennai, or 40% of basic for every other city (including Bengaluru, Hyderabad and Pune); or rent paid minus 10% of basic. You must pay rent and submit receipts, plus landlord PAN if annual rent exceeds ₹1 lakh. HRA exemption is not available under the new tax regime.

What is the additional NPS deduction under Section 80CCD(1B)?

Section 80CCD(1B) allows an additional deduction of up to ₹50,000 for NPS contributions, over and above the ₹1.5 lakh 80C limit, old regime only. Contribute via payroll or eNPS. For someone actually in the 30% old-regime slab, the full ₹50,000 saves about ₹15,600 including cess.

Can I switch between old and new tax regime every year?

Salaried employees can usually switch each financial year by informing the employer at the start of the year or on joining. The ITR locks the regime for that year. People with business income who opt out of the new regime face tighter switch-back rules. Recalculate every April — 87A in FY 2026-27 moved the breakeven versus older articles that still say “old from ₹8 lakh.”
